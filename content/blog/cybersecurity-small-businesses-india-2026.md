Cybersecurity is no longer a concern only for banks, IT companies and large corporations. In 2026, Indian small businesses — salons, clinics, shops, schools, hotels and D2C brands — are among the most common targets of cybercriminals, not because they hold the most valuable data, but because they are the most exposed.

According to [CERT-In's figures for 2025](https://www.sitewall.net/india-cyber-attack-surface-cert-in-dbir-2026/), India's Computer Emergency Response Team handled roughly 29.44 lakh cyber incidents in 2025, up about 44% from 20.41 lakh incidents in 2024. A [Kaspersky survey cited by Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/amp/technology/tech-news/rising-cyberattacks-push-indian-smes-to-boost-security-spending-report-126091800816_1.html) found that 87% of Indian small and medium businesses surveyed reported a cyber incident in the past year. The business case for basic security has rarely been stronger.

This guide covers the threats that matter for Indian small businesses, a practical checklist you can start this week, the legal obligations to know, and what to do if you are already breached.

## Why do Indian small businesses get targeted?

Attackers often look for the weakest entry point, and small businesses frequently have:

- **A limited security budget.** [Kaspersky and Tata Tele Business Services-CyberMedia data cited by Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/amp/technology/tech-news/rising-cyberattacks-push-indian-smes-to-boost-security-spending-report-126091800816_1.html) show that only about 46% of SMEs allocate less than 5% of their IT budget to cybersecurity, while 84% plan to increase that spending.
- **Shared or reused credentials.** Employees often use the same password across email, banking, social media and cloud tools. Once one platform leaks, attackers try the same password everywhere.
- **Outdated systems and unpatched software.** Office equipment connected to the internet, browsers, WordPress sites, plugins and old laptops running end-of-life operating systems.
- **A single person holding too much access.** One employee may control the website, the domain, the email, the payments account and the customer data at the same time.
- **No tested backup.** Having a backup file saved somewhere is not the same as having a backup that restores cleanly when a ransomware attack or hardware failure hits.

Attackers do not need sophisticated methods. [Phishing — mostly through fake emails — remains the most common initial vector](https://www.ecybertech.com/blog-cybersecurity-indian-businesses-2026), with credential reuse, software vulnerabilities and mass malware following close behind.

## The five threats that matter most

### 1. Phishing and business email compromise (BEC)

A fake email pretends to be your supplier, your CA, your bank or your boss — asking for credentials, an urgent payment or a login link. In 2026 these emails are often written in fluent Hindi and English and are difficult to distinguish from genuine messages.

**What you can do:** train every person who receives email to treat unexpected payment or credential requests as suspicious, even when the message looks convincing. Use business email with AI-based phishing filters (Google Workspace and Microsoft 365 include this) and keep a written verification procedure for payments above a certain amount.

### 2. Credential theft and password reuse

Billions of username-and-password combinations from past breaches are freely available on the dark web, and attackers test them against business login portals automatically.

**What you can do:** use a password manager, turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA) on every important account, and make sure bank and payment accounts never accept passwords or authenticator codes by email.

### 3. Ransomware

Ransomware is a form of malicious software that blocks access to files and demands payment to restore them. Reports for India describe the average ransom demand as crossing ₹35 lakh. An attack can freeze a clinic's patient records, a shop's stock data or a finance office's invoices.

**What you can do:** keep offline or separate, encrypted daily backups; test a restore at least monthly; keep machines patched; and know that paying a demand does not guarantee recovery — a tested clean backup is the real safety net.

### 4. Unpatched software and plugins

[Recent reports](https://www.kirti.co.in/cyber-attacks-are-targeting-indian-smes-in-2026-heres-how-ai-stops-them/) show that most breaches exploit known, unpatched vulnerabilities — unpatched WordPress themes, browsers, plugins and operating systems. Attackers do not need to invent new exploits.

**What you can do:** enable automatic updates wherever the software allows it; remove unused plugins and themes; and fix known vulnerabilities as soon as they are published, not "when time permits".

### 5. Third-party exposure

Your website, hosting provider, payment gateway, email service and cloud storage all become part of your attack surface. A supplier compromise can expose your customer data even if your own systems are well-maintained.

**What you can do:** use vendors with clear security practices, keep a list of everyone who touches your customer data, and review that list when you accept new tools or services.

## A practical checklist for the next 30 days

Work through this list in order. You do not need enterprise software to start.

1. **Turn on MFA everywhere it is available** — email, banking, domain registrar, website hosting, payment systems and social accounts.
2. **Adopt a password manager and stop password reuse.** No shared accounts, no "same password, different website".
3. **Back up daily and test the restore.** Keep at least one backup offline or in a separate environment, encrypt it, and attempt a restore monthly.
4. **Patch and update systems.** OS, browsers, plugins, themes and internal tools. Remove anything no longer used.
5. **Train staff to spot phishing.** Run a short phishing-awareness drill at least once a year, and make it a genuine, safe routine rather than a single mandatory document.
6. **Limit access by role.** Give each person only the access they need for their job. A cashier does not need admin rights to the company website.
7. **Separate personal and business data.** Keep customer and payment information in systems fit for that purpose, not in personal spreadsheets.
8. **Prepare an incident-response plan** before you need it. See the next section.
9. **Keep a written security owner and contact list.** One person must know who to call, where backups live and where credentials are stored — even if that person is you.

A quick note on tools: do **not** evaluate security products by brand alone. Ask a vendor which standard controls they support (encryption, logging, MFA, access control) and verify the answer in writing.

## The legal side: what Indian businesses need to know

### CERT-In: the 6-hour clock that is already in force

India's CERT-In (Indian Computer Emergency Response Team) [requires qualifying cyber incidents to be reported within six hours](https://support.icompaas.com/compliance/india-dpdp-act-2023) of being noticed, and separately requires ICT system logs to be retained for 180 days within India. This rule already applies to businesses today — a breach does not wait for a DPDP deadline.

### DPDP Act, 2023: consent, data security and penalties

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 is designed to bring private-sector personal-data handling under a consistent framework. The key milestones to know are:

- The rules were notified in November 2025, with definitions and the Data Protection Board provisions coming into force on 13 November 2025.
- Consent Manager registration was set to open on 13 November 2026.
- [All Data Fiduciary duties, rights and penalties are scheduled to commence on 13 May 2027](https://support.icompaas.com/compliance/india-dpdp-act-2023).

The Act covers processing of digital personal data in India and processing abroad connected with offering goods or services to people in India. [Penalties can reach ₹250 crore under the Act](https://www.recordinglaw.com/world-laws/world-data-privacy-laws/india-dpdp-vs-gdpr/), while breach-notification obligations — initial alert without delay, then a detailed report within 72 hours — are expected with the full framework. No minimum-size threshold currently removes a small business from the requirement, so "we are too small" is not a defence.

For 2026, the practical preparation you can do now is: map every system and vendor that touches personal data, review consent flows and privacy notices in plain language, and run a security-safeguards gap assessment.

### GDPR: if you serve Europe

If your business serves EU customers, [GDPR-linked contractual and jurisdictional requirements](https://www.recordinglaw.com/world-laws/world-data-privacy-laws/india-dpdp-vs-gdpr/) may apply alongside India law. GDPR requires security measures appropriate to risk and a 72-hour notification framework for qualifying breaches.

> **Important:** Rules and enforcement dates are evolving. The dates above reflect the DPDP Act, 2023 and DPDP Rules as reported in 2026 — confirm current obligations with a legal professional if your business processes significant customer data.

## How to respond if you are breached

Preparation matters more than speed once the incident begins. Use this sequence:

1. **Confirm and record.** Confirm the incident, open a timestamped record and keep the affected systems, logs and evidence intact.
2. **Contain without deleting.** Isolate affected systems from the network. Do not delete suspicious systems or logs before they are preserved — they are needed for investigation and reporting.
3. **Do not pay a demand immediately.** Acting hastily without evidence can make recovery harder. Get professional help first.
4. **Assess reporting duties.** CERT-In's 6-hour reporting requirement and any DPDP, sectoral, contractual or customer-related notifications may apply.
5. **Restore from validated clean backups.** Do not restore until the initial access path has been addressed, or the attacker can return through the same door.
6. **Communicate honestly.** Customers, staff and partners should be told what happened, what is being done and what they should do — without making unverified claims about what was not compromised.

CERT-In's [India Cybersecurity Reporting portal](https://cert-in.org.in/) is the official source for incident-reporting guidance and advisories.

## A business case that survives a budget review

Security spending is hard to justify against the abstract idea of "being hacked". A stronger case compares the annual cost of basic controls — MFA, backups, patching, employee awareness, incident response — against realistic loss scenarios: lost data, ransom demands, downtime, recovery work and regulatory exposure.

[IBM's India study](https://smestreet.in/cybersecurity/cybersecurity-indian-smes-2026-ransomware-ai-cloud-dpdp-12787971) estimated an average breach cost of ₹25.5 crore across surveyed Indian organisations. That figure is an average — not every small business will face it — but it shows why spending a few thousand rupees a month on basic controls is difficult to argue against.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the DPDP Act in force right now for my small business?

Partially. The Act was passed in 2023, the DPDP Rules were notified in November 2025, and definitions plus Board provisions are in force. Consent Manager registration opens on 13 November 2026, and full Data Fiduciary duties, rights and penalties are scheduled to begin on 13 May 2027. CERT-In's existing 6-hour reporting and 180-day log rules, however, are already in force today.

### Do I need a Data Protection Officer (DPO)?

The Act and rules apply DPO-type obligations primarily to Significant Data Fiduciaries. Small businesses are not automatically required to appoint an individual DPO, but they should have an accountable owner for data questions — often the business owner — and a written process that makes reporting and restoration actionable.

### Can I just pay the ransom and move on?

No. Paying a demand does not guarantee restoration, it may fund the attacker, and it does not remove any reporting duty. Restoring from a tested clean backup and notifying the right authorities is the safer approach. Decision support and professional help are advised before paying anything.

### Are phishing drills worth the time?

Yes. Staff behaviour is the most common failure point, and it is also the cheapest to improve. Small, regular, non-shaming drills outperform a single annual document nobody recalls.

## Final thoughts

Cybersecurity for a small business is not about achieving enterprise-grade perfection. It is about removing the most expensive and common paths to failure: phishing, reused credentials, unpatched systems, missing backups and a missing incident-response plan.

Starting with MFA, password hygiene, tested backups and a written incident plan gives Indian small businesses the strongest return on small investments. The legal picture is evolving — CERT-In's 6-hour rule is already in force, and the DPDP framework is coming into full effect on 13 May 2027 — so preparation is not optional.
